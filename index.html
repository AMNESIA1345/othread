Exhibit A

What Chat Control actually is

Officially the Child Sexual Abuse Regulation (CSAR), first proposed by the European Commission in May 2022. Framed as a child-protection measure, it has become the EU's most contested digital rights fight in a decade.

The regulation would allow — and in earlier drafts, require — messaging services to scan private communications for illegal material before they're sent, including inside end-to-end encrypted apps. Detection would run on-device, comparing your messages, photos, and files against databases of known and, controversially, unknown material using pattern-matching AI.

Two versions have moved on parallel tracks. Chat Control 1.0 is a temporary rule letting platforms voluntarily scan unencrypted messages — it lapsed in April 2026 and was controversially revived on 9 July 2026, now running until 2028. Chat Control 2.0, the permanent regulation, would go further and remains stuck in trilogue negotiations between the Council, Parliament, and Commission.

Chat Control 1.0 — Live

Voluntary

Platforms may scan unencrypted messages for known CSAM. In force again since 9 July 2026, running until 3 April 2028. Does not apply to encrypted messages.

Chat Control 2.0 — Pending

Mandatory

Would apply detection orders broadly, including pressure on encrypted services. Blocked in trilogue since June 2026; talks resume September 2026.

Exhibit B

The case against it

#0thread doesn't dispute the goal of protecting children — we dispute the method. Mass, suspicionless scanning trades everyone's privacy for a detection system experts say doesn't reliably work.

01

There's no such thing as a safe backdoor

Any scanning mechanism built into an encrypted app is a vulnerability that can be found, repurposed, or exploited — by criminals, hostile states, or future governments with different intentions.

02

No suspicion required

Detection applies to everyone by default, not people under investigation. It reverses a legal principle that surveillance follows suspicion, not the other way around.

03

False positives at EU scale

Independent technical assessments — including the Parliament's own impact study — found no detection method accurate enough to run across hundreds of millions of users without large numbers of innocent messages being flagged.

04

It sets a precedent

Once infrastructure for mass scanning exists for one category of content, expanding its scope becomes a policy choice, not an engineering problem. Civil liberties groups warn today's CSAM filter is tomorrow's general-purpose scanner.

05

It targets the wrong layer

Offenders who actually want to evade detection can switch to tools outside the regulation's reach. The people left exposed are the hundreds of millions of ordinary users who can't opt out of the apps they rely on for work and family.

06

Chilling effect on speech

Knowing every message might be machine-read changes what people are willing to say — to a doctor, a journalist, a lawyer, an abuse survivor. Privacy isn't only about hiding wrongdoing; it's what makes honest conversation possible.

Exhibit C

How we got here

A condensed record of the fight, from proposal to the present holding pattern.

MAY 2022

The Commission proposes CSAR

The regulation is introduced, immediately drawing criticism from cryptographers, the EDPS, and digital rights groups over its scanning provisions.

2021 – 2024

Chat Control 1.0 extended twice

The temporary voluntary-scanning derogation is repeatedly extended while the permanent regulation stalls in negotiation.

MARCH 2026

Parliament rejects another extension

MEPs vote against extending the temporary regime, allowing the legal basis for voluntary scanning to lapse on 3 April.

APRIL – JUNE 2026

Trilogue without agreement

Negotiators meet repeatedly on the permanent regulation. The fifth trilogue, on 29 June, ends without a deal on scope, mandatory scanning, or encryption safeguards.

9 JULY 2026

Chat Control 1.0 revived

In a fast-tracked vote, Parliament reinstates voluntary scanning until 2028 — despite more MEPs voting against it (314) than for it (276), short of the absolute majority needed to block it.

SEPT 2026

Sixth trilogue — next test

Talks on the permanent regulation resume under the incoming Council presidency. The core dispute — blanket scanning versus targeted, judge-approved detection — remains unresolved.
